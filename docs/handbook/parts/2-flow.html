Why a hub, and why two cities

A hub is a micro-warehouse: a small room of shelves a few kilometres from the homes it serves. It is where quality is checked, where kilograms become units, and where "in stock" is kept true.

Farmers deal in kilograms. Customers buy fixed units: a 500 g pack, a piece, a bunch. The hub converts one into the other, once, at its dock, which keeps every stock count a whole number.

What an order goes through

The customer seesSet when
Placed, ConfirmedThe customer pays for a held cart. Payment is a stand-in, so the two are one moment.
PackedThe picker packs the bag into a crate slot. Only now does stock on hand go down.
PooledThe order is put on a trip. The four-digit code appears on the customer's screen.
Out for deliveryThe agent has checked every bag of the trip out of the hub.
DeliveredThe agent types in the code the customer reads out at the door.
CancelledThe customer cancels before the order is packed, or the hub could find none of its items.
Delivery failedThe agent could not hand the bag over, and records why.

Each state is written when it changes, with its time. The hub publishes an event for each step, and the order moves forward in the same database transaction as the event, so an order cannot disagree with its pick job or its trip.

2 · System designInside a hub

A hub does three things: it lets good produce in, it knows exactly what is on which shelf, and it never promises a pack it does not have. Each rule below is in the code and has a test.

Letting produce in

  • Which hub. The nearest open hubs within 60 km of the farm are tried nearest first. A hub is passed over if it has no shelves of the item's temperature zone, or no room for the weight.
  • Room counts what is coming. Free room = capacity − produce on the shelves − lots already on their way. Without the last term, ten farmers listing in one hour would all be sent to the same nearly full hub.
  • When. The next morning intake window, 06:00 to 09:00 India time, never before the harvest date.
  • Weigh and grade once. Received kg, rejected kg, a grade and a remark. The farmer is judged on rejected ÷ delivered over 30 days, shown beside each expected drop.
  • Kilograms into units. Weights are whole grams. Accepted grams ÷ pack size = whole packs; what does not fill a pack is recorded as trim. The lot becomes batch L<listing> with use-by date = harvest date + shelf life.
  • Too old is refused. A batch with less than half its shelf life left is recorded and never stocked.
  • A lot is taken in once. Two receivers taking in the same lot at the same moment take turns; the second is told it is done.

Only one module writes stock. Every test that moves stock ends by checking these two lines.

  • Counts. A picker who finds a shelf empty corrects the count on the spot, and a count of that bin is opened. Small differences are approved automatically (under ₹500); larger ones wait for a manager.
  • Dates. Expired batches are written off every hour, by India's date.
  • Who did it. Every staff call is in an audit log, refusals included.

Picking and packing

  • Paying creates the pick job. Its bins are chosen then, oldest batch first.
  • One picker per order. Claiming a job is a guarded update, so two pickers cannot take one order.
  • Busy hours. With 4 or more orders waiting, a picker takes 3 in one walk, one tote each. This is batch picking; section 4 has what it saves.
  • Every unit is scanned. A wrong barcode is refused.
  • Empty shelf. The line moves to another bin, or a substitute of the same kind that is never dearer is offered, or the line is dropped and comes off the bill.
  • Packing commits the sale. Until then a cancel only releases a hold; no count is ever "put back".
  • Stuck orders. No scan for 5 minutes and the order returns to the queue.
  • A crate slot. The packed bag waits in a numbered slot, which the agent's screen later names.

Planning ahead

QuestionHow the hub answers it
How much will sell tomorrow?A daily forecast per item. Three simple models are compared by their measured error and the best is used; reorder points follow from it. It does not yet drive requests to farms.
Which shelf for which item?Slotting: each zone's items are ranked by 14 days of sales and the fast sellers get the bins nearest the packing desk.
Where should the next hub go?Every address lookup is saved by its H3 cell (a hexagon of a grid over the map), served or turned away. The network map picks the sites that would cover the most turned-away customers.
What needs attention now?The ops dashboard lists stalled orders, bags waiting and items out of stock first, then every key figure beside its target.
