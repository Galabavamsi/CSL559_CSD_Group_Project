2 · System designDecisions: stock, the shop, the order

Each decision in a few lines: what is done, why, and what it costs. The longer argument is in docs/DESIGN.md. Settings such as 4 orders, 10 minutes and 30% are starting values chosen by the team, not measurements.

Stock is held while the customer pays

What. The shop shows what is available: units on hand minus units held for other carts. Checkout does not remove stock. It holds it, with one statement per line of the cart that carries its own check:

UPDATE stock_levels
   SET qty_reserved = qty_reserved + :qty
 WHERE store_id = :hub AND sku_id = :item
   AND qty_on_hand - qty_reserved >= :qty
-- 0 rows changed: not enough stock

A hold is the whole cart or nothing and lasts ten minutes. A sweep every 30 seconds gives back what ran out.

Why. The database runs these statements one at a time for a given row, so two carts cannot both take the last pack. A test has twenty buyers race for one unit: one wins.

Cost. Units sit off sale for up to ten minutes behind carts that may never be paid for.

The oldest batch is picked first

What. Paying creates the pick job, and its bins are chosen then: the batch with the earliest use-by date first. Warehouses call this first expiry, first out (FEFO). Stock on hand goes down only when the order is packed.

Why. What is not sold by its use-by date is written off. Picking the oldest batch first gives every batch its best chance of selling in time.

The shop describes the batch that would be picked next

What. For each item the shop adds the facts of the batch the next order would be picked from: grade, harvest date, use-by date and farm. They are read from the farmer's listing and the hub's quality check.

Why one batch. Stock is picked oldest first and a customer cannot choose a batch. Listing several would offer a choice the hub does not honour.

Cost. A large order may get some units from a newer batch: never older than shown, but the grade may differ.

A batch in its last day is sold cheaper

What. When the batch to be picked first has a day of shelf life left or less, its units cost 30% less, in whole rupees. This is the rescue price. An order is priced by the batches actually picked: eight packs of milk with six in their last day are six at ₹20 and two at ₹28.

Why. Produce a day from its date is still good to eat today, and what is not sold is wasted. Pricing by the batches picked keeps it honest: otherwise a customer could order ten at the lower price when three such packs exist.

Not taken. A price that falls gradually, or follows demand. Neither can be explained in one sentence or checked in one test.

The checkout shows the hub's bill, and a price that rose is refused

What. The checkout asks the hub what the held cart would cost if paid this moment, and asks again every 20 seconds. The payment carries the total the customer was shown. If the order would cost more, nothing is paid, the cart stays held, and the app shows the new total (409 price_changed). If it would cost less, it is placed.

Why. A hold sets units aside, not batches, so the last cheaper units go to whoever pays first (the second story at the end of this section). The bill comes from the function that later chooses the bins, so no second copy of the rule can drift.

Why refuse, and not keep the price. Keeping it means holding batches for ten minutes, which takes last-day units off sale when they most need to sell.

An order is written inside the payment

What. Paying confirms the hold, and the order is written in the same database transaction: who, which hub, the address, each line at the price charged.

Why at payment. A hold is a cart that may never be paid. An order for every hold would fill a customer's history with abandoned carts.

Why inside. Written before, an order would be left behind when a payment is refused. Written after, a paid cart would have no order if the server stopped in between.

Cost. Two ids for one purchase: the hold's and the order's.

2 · System designDecisions: zones and delivery

Delivery has to answer five questions: when does a trip leave, which orders are on it, in what order are they dropped, who takes it, and how is the hand-over proved.

A delivery zone is a set of H3 cells

What. H3 is an open grid that covers the earth in hexagons; at resolution 9 a cell is about a tenth of a square kilometre. Each hub serves the cells nearer to it than to any other hub, within its radius. "Which hub serves this address" finds the address's cell and reads one indexed row.

Why. Zones are worked out when a hub opens, not when a customer asks. Opening a hub writes the hub, its bins, its logins and every recalculated zone in one transaction. The same cells are what planning counts in.

A trip leaves when enough orders wait, or one has waited long enough

What. Packed orders wait at the hub. A trip is formed when an agent is free and either 4 orders wait or the oldest has waited 10 minutes. A trip takes at most 6.

Why. This is pooling in one rule: waiting buys fuller trips, and the deadline caps what waiting costs the customer. The hub already picks by the same rule.

Not taken. Fixed slots: the app promises minutes, and a slot would promise "by 6 pm". An optimiser: at a few orders an hour it would choose what the rule chooses.

Cost. With one order an hour, every trip carries one order and pooling saves nothing. The order rate at which pooling starts to pay has not been measured.

Which orders go: the longest wait, and its neighbours by direction

What. When more orders wait than a trip takes, the trip is the order that has waited longest and the orders nearest to it in compass direction from the hub.

Why. A trip that goes north and then south drives past the hub in between. The order that made the trip due is always on it.

Cost. Direction ignores railway lines: in Bhilai two areas in one direction can lie on opposite sides of the railway. The first version of this rule was wrong (the first story at the end of this section).

The order of stops is the best one, found by trying them all

What. Every order of a trip's stops is tried, and the shortest round from the hub and back is kept. Six stops have 720 orders.

Why. For trips this small an exact answer is cheap, and the same answer every time can be tested exactly.

Distances. A leg is the straight line times 1.7: roads measured in Bhilai on 24 November were 1.6 to 1.9 times it. The order of stops comes out right for a few addresses in one direction; the kilometres are an estimate.

Not taken. A maps service's optimiser: the order of stops would then depend on a network call at the moment a trip is formed.

The agent free longest takes the trip, and checks each bag out

What. An agent belongs to one hub and goes on duty in the app. A trip goes to the agent on duty there who has been free longest. The agent checks each bag out by its crate slot; when all are out, the orders are out for delivery.

Why. First come, first served is fair and needs no tuning. The moment a bag leaves the hub is the moment responsibility for it changes hands.

Kept safe by the database. Forming a trip takes a lock per hub, and claiming an agent is a guarded update: a trip cannot have two agents, nor an agent two trips.

The door code is worked out, and stored nowhere

What. Each order has a four-digit code, shown to the customer once the order is on a trip. The customer reads it out at the door and the agent types it in. The server works it out as an HMAC of the order's id: a hash that needs the server's secret key, cut down to four digits.

Why not stored. The customer's app must show the code at any time, so a one-way hash would not do, and the other choice is a readable code in a table. Worked out on request, a copy of the database holds no codes. The agent's screen is never sent it.

Why four digits are enough. A guess is right one time in ten thousand, and five wrong tries lock the hand-over.

Cost. A new server secret changes every outstanding code. A locked code cannot yet be released.

2 · System designDecisions: places, time, staging, maps, payment

Two decisions about who is served, one about time, and three about what the system simulates or leaves out on purpose.

Where nobody delivers, a customer can ask, and it is counted

What. An address no hub serves used to end at "not in our delivery area yet". The screen now offers "Request delivery here". The request is kept as the customer and the H3 cell, never the exact point.

Why. Planning suggests new hubs where turned-away lookups pile up. A lookup is weak evidence. A person who taps a button is saying they would order, so a request counts as five lookups.

Cost. The weight of five is a guess.

A hub at IIT Bhilai, not a wider radius

What. A fourth hub in Bhilai, BH-04, about half a kilometre from the campus at Kutelabhata.

Why. The campus is 4 km from the nearest hub and a hub in Bhilai serves 3 km. The place the demo will be given from was outside every zone.

Why not a wider radius. The radius is the promise of a short ride. Widening it for one address would quietly break it for everyone at the hub's edge.

Checked on staging. BH-04 serves the IIT Bhilai area, 0.66 km from the centre of the customer's H3 cell.

A calendar date is India's date

What. Everything that asks "what is the date today" asks one place, which answers with India's date: UTC plus 330 minutes. Stored times stay in UTC.

Why. The server's clock is UTC, so for the first five and a half hours of every Indian day the server was on yesterday (the third story). A use-by date ends at midnight where the pack is.

Not taken. Setting the container's time zone to India. It would have moved every log line, and the tests, which run in UTC, would not be testing what runs.

Cost. Sales statistics still count a day from midnight UTC.

Payment stays a stand-in

What. Placing an order takes no payment details. Why. A payment gateway needs a merchant account, callbacks and refunds, and proves nothing about micro-warehousing. It is one function, so a gateway can replace it. Cost. Every demonstration has to say that no money moves.

On staging the people are simulated, and the code paths are not

What. Two settings, on for staging and off by default. Demo farms: every half hour, a hub with fewer than six units of a produce item gets a lot from a demo farm. Demo hub: an order no picker has claimed after 45 seconds is claimed, scanned and packed by the server.

Why. A hub sells only what a farm has brought, so Bhilai's new hubs showed an empty shop to the first person who looked. And one person has to show an order from a phone, with nobody at the hub.

Why through the ordinary intake and pick. Stock written straight onto a shelf would have no listing, no quality check and no farm, and that is what the shop exists to show. An order merely marked as packed would leave the stock records describing a hub nobody had touched.

Cost. Staging no longer shows "the shop was empty until the farmer listed".

Maps: one provider on AWS, and a map is never required

What. Decided on 24 November and not yet in the app: map tiles, place search and road times from Amazon Location Service, drawn with MapLibre, an open-source map renderer. Every flow keeps working with no map at all: an area from a list or a GPS fix, and straight-line distances.

Why this provider. It runs in Mumbai beside the rest, on the account and credits the project has, and the design takes the service on AWS where two are equal. Google's terms do not allow its routing or places with someone else's map; it stays the fallback if Amazon's data proves poor.

Why never required. The demo runs on a network the project does not control, which has already blocked the project's own address once.

Tested in Bhilai. Areas were found within about 1 km of points chosen by hand; a street address came back in the wrong sector. So a search finds the area, and the customer will move a pin to the door.

The key for map tiles can do nothing else, because it travels to every phone that draws the map and can be copied. It ends on 31 March 2027.

2 · System designThree rules that were wrong

All three were found on 24 November: one by a test, one by reading code, one by looking at the running system at an hour nobody had looked before.

1. The sweep that sent an agent out with one bag

The ruleWhen more orders wait than a trip can take, the design cut the circle around the hub into fixed wedges and sent the wedge holding the oldest order. This is the textbook "sweep" method for planning vehicle rounds (Gillett and Miller, 1974).
What happenedWith three orders waiting for one building and room for two, the cut fell so that the oldest order was alone in the last wedge. An agent left with one bag while two that could have gone stayed behind.
How it was foundBy the plainest test written for the new code: "a trip takes no more than its most".
What changedThe sweep divides all the orders at once. Here a trip is formed one at a time, as an agent comes free, and that difference was missed when the design was written. The trip is now the longest-waiting order and its nearest neighbours by direction, which always fills the trip.

2. The price that rose unseen

The ruleThe checkout's total was a sum made on the phone from the shop's list. Nothing compared it with what the order then cost.
What could happenA hold sets units aside, not batches. Two customers could each hold six packs while six remained at the rescue price, and both were rightly shown the lower price, because either might pay first. Whoever paid second was charged the full price, on a screen that had said otherwise.
How it was foundBy reading the app's checkout code, while the app was being moved onto the server's orders.
What changedThe checkout shows the hub's own bill. The payment carries the total shown, and an order that would cost more is refused with nothing paid. Eight new tests, one of them two customers and six rescue packs.
Still openUnits the hub would fail to find are counted at full price in the bill and leave the order at payment. In that rare case the order costs less than the bill said.

3. The date fault at one in the morning

The ruleThe code took "today" from the server's clock, which is UTC.
What happenedIndia is five and a half hours ahead, so a pack stayed on sale until 05:30 on the morning after its use-by date. On staging at 01:00 on 24 November, India time, a batch with a use-by date of the 23rd was still on sale, and at the rescue price.
How it was foundBy looking at staging after a deploy, at that hour. The tests could not catch it: they took "today" from the same wrong clock as the code.
What changedOne place answers "what is the date", with India's date. The tests ask it too, and set the clock to both sides of India's midnight. The run that checked the fix happened to fall at 01:43 India time.
Still openSales statistics still count a day from midnight UTC.

Two smaller ones, from the journal. A database migration was edited after it had been applied to a test database; thirteen tests then failed with an error about SQL, not about the change. And a staging setting was edited in the working folder while a deploy of the previous change was under way, and the deploy script read its template from that folder. The edit was taken back out in time. The script has been changed to read the template of the commit it deploys; that step was tried by hand and has not yet run inside a deploy.
