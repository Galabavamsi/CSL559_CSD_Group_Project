1 · The ideaFarm to hub to home

A farmer lists a lot in kilograms. A small local hub weighs it, grades it and packs it into the units customers buy. A customer's phone shows only what their hub can deliver now.

Steps 1 to 7 run today on the real backend, and section 3 follows one lot of tomatoes through them with the numbers above. Step 8, delivery, is the largest part still to build.

Why a hub, and why two cities

A hub is a micro-warehouse: a small room of shelves a few kilometres from the homes it serves. It is where quality is checked, where kilograms become sellable units, and where the promise "this is in stock" is kept. Everything the customer sees is a statement about one hub's shelves.

Farmers deal in kilograms. Customers buy fixed units: a 500 g pack, a piece with a weight range, a bunch. The hub converts one into the other, once, at its dock. This is how the reference apps sell produce, and it keeps every stock count a whole number.

The same code runs both. A dense tier-1 city gets hubs close together, each serving a small radius; a tier-2 city gets fewer hubs with a longer reach. Bangalore has two hubs in the demo data. Bhilai's hubs are meant to be placed from demand by the network-planning tool (section 5) and none is seeded yet.
